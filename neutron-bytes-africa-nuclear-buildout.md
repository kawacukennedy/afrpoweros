# Africa's Nuclear Buildout, by the Numbers

**Suggested dek:** An open, cited dataset now tracks civilian nuclear programs across 20
African countries — one operating, one building, seven preparing, ten exploring.

For decades, the story of nuclear energy in Africa was a short one: a single operating
station in South Africa and, for nearly everyone else, ambition on paper. That story is
changing, and for the first time the change can be measured. AfrPowerOS, an open dataset
that tracks civilian nuclear activity across the continent with every record cited and
confidence-labelled, now covers 20 countries. One operates a commercial plant. One is
building the continent's first new nuclear station in decades. Seven are preparing in
earnest under the IAEA's phased milestones approach, and ten are exploring.

The scale is modest against the world's giants, but the trajectory matters — not least
because Africa is the continent where nuclear is arriving newest and, in several states,
fastest.

## The operating reference point: South Africa

South Africa remains the only African country with an operating commercial nuclear power
plant. Koeberg, near Cape Town, has run two units (1,860 MW net) for decades, and it is now
the anchor for an ambitious expansion. In October 2025, the government approved Integrated
Resource Plan 2025, which calls for 5,200 MW of new nuclear capacity by 2039, with an
initial 1,200 MW targeted for 2036. In November 2025, the regulator extended Koeberg
Unit 2's operating licence to 2045. The procurement route for the new build has not yet
been finalised, which makes South Africa the contest to watch on the continent.

## Africa's first new build in decades: Egypt

Egypt is delivering what no African country has managed in a generation: an actual new
nuclear plant under construction. El Dabaa, on the Mediterranean coast, comprises four
VVER-1200 units supplied by Rosatom under a 2015 intergovernmental agreement backed by a
Russian state loan facility of roughly $25 billion. First concrete was poured in July 2022;
Unit 1 is targeted for grid connection around 2028, with all four units following by about
2030. When connected, 4.8 GW of nuclear capacity will more than triple Africa's installed
nuclear capacity overnight — and give the continent its first real construction template
in decades.

## The preparing tier: racing toward first grid dates

A cluster of countries is moving through the IAEA's three-phase milestones framework,
each set on becoming the next African operator after Egypt.

**Kenya** completed Phase 1 in 2018, progressed site evaluation with an IAEA SEED mission,
and in March 2026 announced a target of first nuclear generation by 2034, with roughly
2,000 MW of capacity planned. Site and vendor selection remain open.

**Ghana**, the continent's first research-reactor operator (GHARR-1), completed Phase 1 in
2017 and in March 2025 named finalist vendors for a first plant of about 1 GW.

**Rwanda** has advanced furthest among East African Phase 2 countries, completing an IAEA
INIR Phase 2 review and follow-up mission in March 2026 and signing a phased roadmap with
Russia in 2025 aimed at an initial nuclear phase around 2030, including a first-of-kind
Dual Fluid Energy reactor feasibility study.

**Uganda** and **Tanzania** complete the middle tier. Uganda's Vision 2040 targets 24 GW by
2040, and the initial ~1,000 MW of the Buyende NPP is targeted by 2031 — on a site planned
for ~8,400 MW with phased commissioning; it entered Phase 2 in 2023. Tanzania finished Phase
1 in 2019, completed its INIR Phase 2 review in 2023, and has identified Namtumbo as a
candidate site, with a 2026 readiness assessment.

**Nigeria**, the continent's most populous country with a large grid but low electricity
access, completed Phase 1 in 2009 and in 2025 renewed small modular reactor interest with
vendors and the IAEA, alongside a 2024 cooperation MoU with China.

## The exploring tier: new reasons to look at nuclear

Below the preparing tier, a dozen countries are at the exploration stage — several for
reasons that have nothing to do with prestige.

**Zambia** is the clearest example. A severe drought in 2024/2025 exposed how much of its
power depends on hydro; in 2025 the government announced it would evaluate about 1 GW of
nuclear for baseload. For states that have watched climate stress batter their grids,
nuclear's selling point is no longer exotic. Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Ethiopia, Senegal,
Niger, Mali, Zimbabwe and Eswatini round out a study tier that could quietly convert to
preparation as feasibility work and financing mature.

## More than power: research, regulation and readiness

The dataset also tracks the earlier rungs of nuclear infrastructure. DR Congo has no
commercial program, but in January 2026 the IAEA approved a technical cooperation project
to restart the TRICO-II research reactor in Kinshasa, supporting applications in health,
agriculture and mining. Every tracked country maintains a regulator or national contact
point, and records cover research reactors, implementing agencies, vendors, agreements
and electricity access — the institutional skeleton that determines whether a program is
real or aspirational.

## Why open data matters

None of this is easy to follow from a single source: programs are announced in national
plans, IAEA reports, vendor releases and trade press at different cadences and with
different degrees of reliability. AfrPowerOS addresses that by making every record
verifiable — each entry carries its source URL and a confidence label, so a reader can
distinguish a documented fact from a report or a hypothesis. The full dataset is public,
machine-readable and free.

The picture it paints is coherent. One African country operates, one is building, seven
are preparing, ten exploring — and the pacing, from the 2034 Kenya target to Egypt's 2028
Unit 1, suggests the continent's nuclear chapter is no longer hypothetical. It is a
construction schedule.
