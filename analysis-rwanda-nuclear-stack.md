# Rwanda's Nuclear Stack in One Page

**AfrPowerOS Analysis · August 2026 · by Kennedy Kawacu**

---

## The short version

Rwanda is the furthest-advanced IAEA Phase 2 country in East Africa. It completed
an INIR mission in March 2026, signed agreements with both Rosatom and Dual Fluid
Energy, and targets a first small modular reactor (SMR) operational by the early
2030s. Nuclear power is planned to supply 60–70% of Rwanda's future electricity
mix. No procurement decision has been made yet.

The IAEA team identified "strong government commitment" and "proactive engagement
with stakeholders" as good practices. Recommendations include finalizing the
national report, completing the legislative review, and adopting supporting policies.

### Vendor relationships

Rwanda has signed agreements with two vendors — a deliberate hedging strategy:

**1. Dual Fluid Energy (Canada/Germany)**
- Agreement signed: September 2023
- What: Critical Demonstration Experiment (CDE) — a functional prototype of the
  DF300 SMR using liquid fuel and lead coolant
- Status: Site licensing 90%+ complete; construction targeted for 2026; testing
  phase 2028–2030
- Cost: €70 million ($75M), financed by Dual Fluid
- Significance: If successful, this would be the world's first "Generation V" reactor
- Safety review: Paul Scherrer Institute (Switzerland) has reviewed the safety case
- Material testing: ANSTO (Australia) and NCBJ (Poland) supporting development
- Training: Rwandan scientists at University of Rwanda receiving advanced reactor
  technology training

**2. Rosatom (Russia)**
- Agreement signed: 2025 (phased roadmap)
- What: Joint coordination committee for phased nuclear energy cooperation
- Target: Initial nuclear phase by ~2030
- Status: Roadmap signed, no procurement decision

**3. French collaboration**
- What: Training and research facilities
- Status: Under discussion

**Why nuclear matters here:** Rwanda's peak demand is roughly half its installed
capacity, but demand is growing at 10% per year. At that rate, demand doubles
every 7 years. The REG Strategic Plan (2024–2034) targets 1,066 MW by 2034 —
roughly 2.3× current capacity. Nuclear would fill a significant portion of that
gap, especially for baseload power that hydro and solar cannot provide during
dry seasons.

## What "60–70% nuclear" actually means

President Kagame stated that Rwanda aims for nuclear to supply 60–70% of its
energy mix. For context:

- If Rwanda's 2034 demand reaches 1,066 MW (REG target), 60–70% nuclear would
  mean 640–746 MW of nuclear capacity
- A single SMR (like the Dual Fluid DF300 or a Rosatom VVER-500) could provide
  50–500 MW depending on design
- Rwanda would likely need 2–4 SMR units to reach that target
- No procurement decision has been made; the Dual Fluid CDE is a prototype, not
  a commercial reactor

**Important caveat:** Government announcements ≠ projects. The 60–70% target is
a policy aspiration, not a procurement commitment. The IAEA INIR team noted that
Rwanda still needs to "finalize the comprehensive report to support the national
decision-making to introduce nuclear power."

## What to watch next

1. **Dual Fluid CDE construction start** — the most concrete near-term signal.
   If construction begins in 2026 as planned, Rwanda becomes the first African
   country to build an advanced reactor prototype.

2. **INIR follow-up recommendations** — the IAEA and Rwanda will develop an
   "integrated workplan" based on the March 2026 mission. This will define the
   next steps and timeline.

3. **Rosatom procurement decision** — the 2025 roadmap is a framework, not a
   contract. Watch for any announcement of a specific reactor type or capacity.

4. **Nuclear law completion** — the IAEA recommended completing the legislative
   review. Without a nuclear law, no commercial reactor can be licensed.

5. **Demand growth** — if Rwanda's electricity demand continues at 10%/year, the
   economic case for nuclear becomes stronger. If demand slows, the timeline
   may extend.
