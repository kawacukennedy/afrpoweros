# The 19 IAEA Milestones Explained

**AfrPowerOS Guide · August 2026 · by Kennedy Kawacu**

---

## What is the Milestones Approach?

The IAEA Milestones Approach is the international standard for countries building
their first nuclear power plant. It splits the work into **3 phases**, marked by
**3 milestones**, covering **19 infrastructure issues**. The process typically
takes 10–15 years from initial consideration to first reactor operation.

This guide explains each of the 19 issues in plain language, with African
examples where applicable.

---

## The three phases

| Phase | Question answered | Typical duration |
|-------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Phase 1** — Consider | "Why nuclear? Should we do it?" | 2–4 years |
| **Phase 2** — Prepare | "How do we build the infrastructure?" | 3–5 years |
| **Phase 3** — Implement | "How do we construct and operate the plant?" | 3–6 years |

Each phase ends with a **milestone** — a checkpoint where progress is assessed
before moving forward.

| Milestone | What it means |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Milestone 1** | Ready to make a knowledgeable commitment to nuclear power |
| **Milestone 2** | Ready to invite bids or negotiate a contract for the first plant |
| **Milestone 3** | Ready to operate the first nuclear power plant |

## The 19 infrastructure issues

### 1. National position

**What it is:** The government's strategic decision on why nuclear power fits
into the national energy plan.

**Phase 1:** Conduct a pre-feasibility study. Answer: why nuclear? What role
does it play? What are the alternatives?

**Phase 2:** Formalize the decision. Establish a coordinating body (NEPIO).
Commit resources.

**Phase 3:** Maintain political commitment through construction.

**African context:** Rwanda completed its Phase 1 assessment. The government
targets 60–70% nuclear in the future energy mix.

---

### 2. Nuclear safety

**What it is:** Ensuring the safety case for the plant is sound — including
site evaluation, design safety, and safety analysis.

**Phase 1:** Identify safety requirements. Understand IAEA safety standards.

**Phase 2:** Develop safety criteria for site and design selection. Conduct
preliminary safety assessments.

**Phase 3:** Complete detailed safety analysis. Submit to regulatory review.

**African context:** The Paul Scherrer Institute (Switzerland) reviewed the
Dual Fluid reactor safety case for Rwanda's CDE.

---

### 3. Management

**What it is:** Establishing the organizational structure — who manages the
program, who operates the plant, who regulates it.

**Phase 1:** Identify key organizations. Create a NEPIO (Nuclear Energy
Programme Implementing Organization).

**Phase 2:** Establish the owner/operator. Define roles between government,
regulator, and operator.

**Phase 3:** Operator achieves full readiness. Management systems in place.

**African context:** Rwanda established RAEB (nuclear authority) in 2020. REG
controls electricity generation and would likely be the operator.

---

### 4. Funding and financing

**What it is:** How the program will be paid for — government budget, private
investment, export credit, international financing.

**Phase 1:** Estimate costs. Identify potential financing models.

**Phase 2:** Secure financing commitments. Develop financial projections.

**Phase 3:** Finalize financing. Manage cost overruns during construction.

**African context:** Dual Fluid's CDE is financed by Dual Fluid itself (€70M).
Rosatom typically offers vendor financing. Rwanda's nuclear program cost is
estimated at $2–5B for a full-scale deployment.

---

### 5. Legal framework

**What it is:** The laws governing nuclear energy — nuclear law, radiation
protection law, liability conventions, and regulatory authority.

**Phase 1:** Review existing legislation. Identify gaps.

**Phase 2:** Enact nuclear law. Establish legal basis for regulatory body.
Join relevant international conventions (Vienna Convention, Paris Convention).

**Phase 3:** Laws fully operational. Liability framework in place.

**African context:** Rwanda's nuclear law is draft under review. The IAEA
INIR team recommended completing the legislative review.

---

### 6. Safeguards

**What it is:** Ensuring nuclear material is not diverted from peaceful use.
Involves IAEA inspections, declarations, and the Additional Protocol.

**Phase 1:** Understand safeguards obligations. Sign the NPT and safeguards
agreement.

**Phase 2:** Conclude safeguards agreements. Implement the Additional Protocol.
Prepare for inspections.

**Phase 3:** Full safeguards implementation. IAEA inspection regime operational.

**African context:** Most African nuclear countries have NPT and comprehensive
safeguards agreements. The Additional Protocol varies.

---

### 7. Regulatory framework

**What it is:** The independent regulatory body that oversees nuclear safety —
licensing, inspection, enforcement.

**Phase 1:** Assess regulatory needs. Identify institutional options.

**Phase 2:** Establish or designate the regulatory body. Develop regulations.
Ensure independence from the operator.

**Phase 3:** Regulatory body fully staffed and operational. Licensing process
complete.

**African context:** South Africa's National Nuclear Regulator (NNR) is the
most established in Africa. Rwanda's regulatory framework is under development.

---

### 8. Radiation protection

**What it is:** Protecting workers, the public, and the environment from
radiation exposure. Includes dose limits, monitoring, and waste management.

**Phase 1:** Identify requirements. Understand IAEA safety standards.

**Phase 2:** Develop regulations. Establish monitoring capabilities.

**Phase 3:** Full radiation protection program operational.

---

### 9. Electrical grid

**What it is:** The power grid must be able to absorb the output of a nuclear
plant — typically 500–1,000 MW for large reactors, 50–300 MW for SMRs.

**Phase 1:** Assess grid capacity. Identify minimum grid size.

**Phase 2:** Plan grid upgrades if needed. Study grid stability impacts.

**Phase 3:** Grid ready to accept nuclear power. Transmission infrastructure
in place.

**African context:** Rwanda's grid peak is ~234 MW. A large reactor (1,000 MW)
would overwhelm it. SMRs (50–300 MW) are the realistic option. REG targets
1,066 MW by 2034.

---

### 10. Human resource development

**What it is:** Training enough nuclear engineers, operators, regulators, and
support staff to run the program.

**Phase 1:** Assess current workforce. Identify training needs.

**Phase 2:** Establish training programs. Send staff abroad for training.
Partner with universities.

**Phase 3:** Local training capacity operational. Operators certified.

**African context:** Rwandan scientists are training at University of Rwanda
and with Dual Fluid. CMU-Africa offers energy courses. South Africa's necsa
has the continent's deepest nuclear workforce.

---

### 11. Stakeholder engagement

**What it is:** Communicating with the public, media, and stakeholders about
the nuclear program. Building public acceptance.

**Phase 1:** Identify stakeholders. Begin public awareness campaigns.

**Phase 2:** Regular engagement. Address public concerns. Develop communication
strategy.

**Phase 3:** Ongoing engagement through construction and operation.

---

### 12. Site and supporting facilities

**What it is:** Selecting and characterizing the site for the nuclear plant —
including seismic, hydrological, meteorological, and population studies.

**Phase 1:** Identify potential sites. Preliminary assessments.

**Phase 2:** Detailed site characterization. Site selection decision.

**Phase 3:** Site preparation. Construction begins.

**African context:** Rwanda completed a site suitability survey in April 2024
with positive results. Dual Fluid's CDE site licensing is 90%+ complete.

---

### 13. Environmental protection

**What it is:** Assessing and mitigating the environmental impact of the
nuclear plant. Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) requirements.

**Phase 1:** Understand requirements. Identify potential impacts.

**Phase 2:** Conduct EIA. Obtain environmental permits.

**Phase 3:** Environmental monitoring during construction and operation.

---

### 14. Emergency preparedness and response

**What it is:** Planning for nuclear emergencies — evacuation plans, alert
systems, coordination with emergency services.

**Phase 1:** Identify requirements. Understand IAEA standards.

**Phase 2:** Develop emergency plans. Train responders. Establish coordination.

**Phase 3:** Emergency response capabilities fully operational.

---

### 15. Nuclear security

**What it is:** Protecting nuclear material and facilities from theft, sabotage,
and terrorism. Distinct from safety — security is about deliberate threats.

**Phase 1:** Understand requirements. Assess current security capabilities.

**Phase 2:** Develop security plans. Implement physical protection systems.

**Phase 3:** Full security regime operational. Regular drills.

---

### 16. Nuclear fuel cycle

**What it is:** The full lifecycle of nuclear fuel — uranium mining, enrichment,
fuel fabrication, use in the reactor, and spent fuel storage/disposal.

**Phase 1:** Understand fuel cycle options. Identify supply arrangements.

**Phase 2:** Secure fuel supply agreements. Plan for spent fuel management.

**Phase 3:** Fuel supply chain operational. Spent fuel management plan in place.

**African context:** Africa has uranium deposits in Niger, Namibia, South Africa,
and Tanzania. Most African nuclear programs would import enriched fuel.

---

### 17. Radioactive waste management

**What it is:** Managing all radioactive waste — from low-level waste during
construction to spent fuel and high-level waste.

**Phase 1:** Understand requirements. Identify waste management options.

**Phase 2:** Develop waste management strategy. Plan for interim storage.

**Phase 3:** Waste management facilities operational.

---

### 18. Industrial involvement

**What it is:** Developing local industry capacity to support construction,
operation, and maintenance of the nuclear plant.

**Phase 1:** Assess current industrial capabilities. Identify gaps.

**Phase 2:** Develop local supply chains. Build partnerships with vendors.

**Phase 3:** Local industry participating in construction and operation.

**African context:** This is one of the biggest challenges for African nuclear
programs. Local industrial capacity for nuclear-grade components is limited
outside South Africa.

---

### 19. Procurement

**What it is:** The process of selecting a vendor and reactor design, negotiating
contracts, and managing the procurement process.

**Phase 1:** Understand procurement options. Identify potential vendors.

**Phase 2:** Prepare tender documents. Evaluate bids. Negotiate contracts.

**Phase 3:** Contract signed. Construction management.

**African context:** Ghana, Kenya, and Rwanda are all in Phase 2 — evaluating
vendors before making procurement decisions. South Africa is the only African
country to have completed a procurement cycle (Koeberg, 1980s).
